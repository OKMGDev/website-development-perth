## How Perth customers choose a trade business

A homeowner with a leaking roof, a kitchen that needs a redo, or a fence that failed in a storm is not reading brand manifestos. They want to know what you actually do, whether you work in their suburb, and how to ask for a quote without a 14-field form. We wrote a practical structure guide — [what makes a good website for Perth tradies](/blog/website-for-tradies-guide/) — and the same logic applies to builders, civil crews and specialist contractors.

Referrals still get checked. The person who was given your name will search it on a phone, skim three photos, and decide whether you look like a business they can let into the house. If the site is a Facebook page with no service list, you lose jobs you never hear about.

## Homeowner, strata and commercial paths

Not every enquiry is a weekend homeowner. Strata managers want a clear trade list, insurance and licence details you already hold, and a way to send a job through without calling during a pour. Commercial and builder clients want capability, safety documents you are willing to share, and a project contact — not a “call now for 10% off” banner.

Those audiences should not share a single generic “services” blob. A bathroom renovation page and a commercial maintenance page answer different questions. [Custom website design](/services/custom-website-design/) is often the right starting point because the information architecture has to match how you quote, not how a theme vendor grouped “about / gallery / contact”.

Emergency or priority work (storm damage, no hot water, unsafe structures) needs a visible phone number and a short “what to tell us” list. Planned work can afford a form that captures job type, suburb, timing and photos.

## Services, suburbs and proof that does not clutter

Breadth is the classic tradie-site problem: you do twenty job types, so the homepage tries to say all twenty at once. The fix is a short list of primary trades on the home page, then dedicated pages for the jobs you actually want more of. Secondary work can live as related links, not as equal-weight buttons.

Service areas should be honest. If you cover [Osborne Park](/locations/osborne-park/) through to [Midland](/locations/midland/) and down to [Rockingham](/locations/rockingham/), say so. If a suburb is a stretch, do not list it to win a search you will then decline. Customers notice when the van never comes.

Proof is photos of completed work, not invented testimonials or dollar amounts. Before-and-after pairs, a line on the job type, and the suburb (when the client is comfortable) beat a slideshow of stock interiors. Show credentials you already hold — licences, memberships, insurance — as facts, not as a guarantee of any outcome.

## Quote requests that work on a phone at a job

A large share of enquiries happen on a phone: yours, on site, with dirty hands; or theirs, standing in the room that needs the work. The quote form should be thumb-friendly: job type, suburb, preferred contact method, and an optional photo upload. Required fields that ask for a company ABN and a desktop-only file picker will be abandoned.

Click-to-call should stay visible. Some customers will not type. After-hours visitors still convert if the page says when you return calls and what information to leave. Do not promise attendance times the diary cannot support.

## Referrals, ads and the pages that support both

The website’s job in a referral-led business is confirmation: you are real, you do this job, you work here, here is how to ask. When you do run ads, do not send paid traffic to that same homepage. A [landing page](/services/landing-page-design/) for a single trade matches the search and asks for one action. [SEO](/services/seo-services/) for trades is usually service-plus-suburb pages written like a human quotes, not a keyword list.

Most owners do not have a copywriter on staff. That is expected. We draft from how you already talk about jobs: what you say on the phone, what is in old quotes, and what is on the camera roll.
