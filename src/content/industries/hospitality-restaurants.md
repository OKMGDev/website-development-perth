## The “where should we eat” decision on a phone

Most restaurant, cafe, bar and venue traffic is a group decision happening on a small screen. Someone has a name, a suburb, or a craving. They need hours, a sense of the room, whether they can book, and enough menu detail to settle dietary questions. If that page takes too long to load, the group picks the place whose site opened first.

That is true in [Fremantle](/locations/fremantle/) on a Saturday and in [Subiaco](/locations/subiaco/) on a weeknight. Hospitality websites fail less from a lack of personality than from slow images, buried menus, and a booking button that sits below a video autoplay.

## Diners, functions and regulars — three different jobs

Walk-in and bookable dining is one journey: menu, hours, location, parking or public transport, and a reservation action. Functions and events are another: capacity, spaces, a sample run of show, and an enquiry form that captures date, guest count and dietary notes. Regulars still use the site for changed hours, public-holiday service, and “are you doing that collab again”.

Treating those as one homepage story creates clutter. A diner choosing tonight does not need a 400-word brand essay before the menu. A wedding enquiry does not need tonight’s specials as the first screen. Separate templates keep each path short.

Cafes with retail (beans, merch) and venues with tickets add a third conversion type. Those should not hijack the book-a-table action. One primary action per template, with secondary links in a predictable place.

## Menus, dietary notes and multi-venue clarity

Menu updates are the operational reality. If the only way to change a dish is a developer ticket, the live menu will be wrong. We usually set menus up as structured content the venue can edit, or as a designed PDF or image the team replaces — whichever matches how often the pass actually changes. Neither approach should require rebuilding the site.

Dietary notes should be clear and modest. List what you can state with confidence. Do not turn the website into a medical or allergen advisory service. Invite guests with complex requirements to call or note them on the booking.

Multi-location groups need a location chooser that does not mix suburbs, hours and menus into one scrolling muddle. Each venue page should carry its own menu, booking path, parking notes and photos. A group homepage can tell the story; it should not be the only place hours live.

## Bookings, walk-ins and why speed matters

If you use a reservation product, the site should explain the room and hand people into that tool — not compete with it. If you take walk-ins only, say so, and show peak times honestly rather than implying a table that does not exist. Functions should go to a human, with a form that captures enough to reply.

Slow sites kill bookings because the decision is competitive and impatient. Compress images, avoid ornamental animation on the booking path, and keep third-party scripts on a leash. A [website redesign](/services/website-redesign/) is often the right move when the current site was built around desktop photography and never checked on a phone over cellular.

## Atmosphere, photography and honest presentation

Hospitality is visual, but stock plates from another hemisphere do not help. Guests compare the site to the room they walk into. Photograph the actual dining room, the courtyard at the hour you want to sell, and dishes you still serve. Galleries should load in stages, not as a twelve-megapixel dump.

[Webflow](/services/webflow-development/) suits many venues because the design can feel branded while editors still change events and seasonal notes. If you are weighing CMS options, [Webflow vs WordPress](/blog/webflow-vs-wordpress/) is written around who will update the site, not around which tool is fashionable.

## Instagram, constant changes and who owns updates

“Instagram is enough” works until someone needs a link they can text to a table of six, a journalist, or a function planner. Social posts expire. Hours, menus and booking links need a stable URL. Use Instagram to attract; use the website to confirm.

Constant menu and event changes are a [maintenance](/services/website-maintenance/) problem as much as a build problem. Decide who on the floor or in the office is allowed to edit, and give them a short path that cannot accidentally break the homepage.
