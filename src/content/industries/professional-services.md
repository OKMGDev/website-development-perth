## How professional buyers actually research a firm

A referred client still checks the website. So does the in-house contact comparing two firms after a board paper, and the founder who searched for a specialist in [Subiaco](/locations/subiaco/) or [Osborne Park](/locations/osborne-park/) at 9pm. They are not looking for a stock skyline and the word “excellence”. They want to know whether you handle their problem, who they will meet, and how a first conversation works.

Legal, accounting, advisory, consulting and similar practices sell judgement. The site has to make that judgement legible without turning every page into a disclaimer — or into a slogan. [Custom website design](/services/custom-website-design/) is usually the right frame because the page map has to follow practice groups and sectors, not a generic theme.

## Partner-led journeys and sector pages that earn their keep

Buying is often partner-led. A visitor may arrive on a named person’s profile from LinkedIn, a conference, or a referral email. That profile should connect to the work they actually do, not to a firm-wide brochure. From there, the path is typically a service page, a sector page, then a consultation request.

Sector pages earn their keep when the work is genuinely different — for example private clients versus operating businesses, or property versus employment matters. They fail when they are the same three paragraphs with the industry noun swapped. If you cannot say what changes for that sector, you probably do not need the page yet.

Multiple offices need more than a footer address. Each location should show who sits there and how to make contact, without implying a shopfront that does not take walk-ins.

## Explaining abstract services in plain language

Abstract services are hard because the client does not buy a widget. They buy a process and a judgement. The page should answer: who this is for, what typically triggers an engagement, what the first meeting covers, and what you will need from them. Describe process, not predicted legal or financial outcomes.

Avoid hollow intensifiers. “Full-service”, “bespoke” and “trusted advisers” are invisible. Specificity is not a claim about results; it is a description of scope. If a service is only offered to existing clients, say so. If a matter type is referred out, say so. Visitors would rather know than enquire into a dead end.

We do not provide legal, tax or financial advice on the website, and we will not draft copy that invents regulatory detail. Claims stay inside what partners will approve. Where professional advertising rules apply to your firm, your own advisers remain the source of truth.

## Credentials, insights and claims you can defend

Team pages should carry credentials you already publish: admission, memberships, languages, and the sectors people actually work in. Photos should look like the people clients will meet. Insights and articles help [SEO](/services/seo-services/) when they are written for a real reader — a process explainer, a change in practice, a checklist — not as keyword wrapping.

Do not publish testimonials, win rates, or matter values unless the firm has already decided that material is appropriate. Invented case studies and implied outcomes are worse than a quiet site.

PDF-heavy archives are common. Brochures can be mined for explanations, then retired as the primary experience. Keep PDFs for documents that should stay downloadable. Do not make the entire website a file cabinet.

## Consultation paths and confidential enquiries

Conversion is usually a confidential enquiry or a booked consultation, not a cart. Some visitors will not use a public calendar; they want a form or a phone number. Others, especially for scoped advisory, will book a timeslot if the page has already qualified what the meeting is for.

Forms should ask for enough to route the matter (practice group, urgency, how they found you) without demanding a case history in the first step. Make it obvious where the message goes. A generic “info@” with no expected response time creates avoidable anxiety in a sector that sells discretion.
