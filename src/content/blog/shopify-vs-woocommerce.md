Shopify and WooCommerce can both sell products well. The better choice for a Perth retailer is rarely about who lists more features. It is about who will run the store, how unusual your catalogue and fulfilment really are, and whether you want a hosted commerce platform or a WordPress site that also takes orders.

If you need a marketing site rather than a cart, read [Webflow vs WordPress for Perth marketing sites](/blog/webflow-vs-wordpress/) instead.

## The real difference is operations, not logos

Shopify is a hosted commerce platform. Hosting, core checkout and a large share of security patching sit with Shopify. You still configure products, shipping, payments, theme sections and apps — but you are not maintaining a server stack.

WooCommerce is a WordPress plugin. The store lives on hosting you (or your agency) control. That is powerful when publishing and commerce need to share one CMS, or when catalogue logic is unusual. It also means updates, backups, PHP versions and plugin conflicts become part of running the business.

Neither option is “more professional”. They fail in different ways. Shopify projects stall on app sprawl, theme limits and operational mismatch. WooCommerce projects stall on hosting neglect, plugin pile-ups and nobody owning updates.

## Choose Shopify when

Shopify is usually the calmer path when:

- You want a hosted platform with predictable commerce primitives: products, collections, cart, checkout and orders.
- Nobody on the team wants to own server maintenance.
- Your catalogue is largely standard — variants, collections, discounts, and common Australian payment and shipping setups.
- You would rather constrain custom behaviour than maintain a bespoke cart.
- You are replacing a marketplace-only presence and need a storefront that merchandises your brand, not only a product grid.

That pattern shows up often for retailers in mixed precincts such as [Fremantle](/locations/fremantle/), where brand, hours and click-and-collect matter as much as the feed of SKUs.

## Choose WooCommerce when

WooCommerce is usually the better fit when:

- You already run WordPress and the store must live inside that editorial system.
- Catalogue, membership, bundling or B2B pricing logic is genuinely unusual.
- Product pages need long-form guides, resources or gated material that a theme-section model will fight.
- You want the option to change hosts, control more of the stack, or keep commerce and publishing tightly intertwined.
- A developer or [maintenance plan](/services/website-maintenance/) will actually look after plugins, backups and PHP — not “we will look at it later”.

WooCommerce is not cheaper by default. You may avoid a Shopify subscription, then spend the difference on hosting quality, plugin licences and care. Total cost of ownership is an operations question, not a monthly-fee slogan. For how those drivers show up in quotes, see [how much a website costs in Perth](/blog/website-cost-perth/).

## A practical decision framework

Work through these in order. Stop when the answer is obvious.

1. **Who ships and who supports?** If orders, refunds and “where is my parcel?” sit with a small team, prefer the platform that reduces moving parts.
2. **How non-standard is the catalogue?** If you cannot explain pricing or fulfilment in a short paragraph, WooCommerce — or a [web application](/services/web-application-development/) — may be safer than forcing apps to impersonate custom software.
3. **Where does content live?** Brand journalism, recipes, education and resources often sit more naturally in WordPress. Pure merchandising often sits more naturally in Shopify.
4. **What happens on a Saturday night?** Hosted platforms fail less often from neglected updates. Self-hosted stores fail more often from deferred maintenance.
5. **What are you willing to own?** Shopify: platform rules, app choices, theme constraints. WooCommerce: hosting, security, plugin compatibility, upgrade cadence.

Write those answers down before you compare themes. Theme galleries are a poor proxy for operations.

### Quick checklist before you brief anyone

- Named owner for products, orders and refunds
- Honest count of product *types*, not only SKU count
- Payment methods you already use, and any you refuse
- Shipping rules for metro Perth, regional WA and the rest of Australia
- Click-and-collect or in-store pickup, if it is real
- Wholesale or member pricing, if it is real
- Whether blog, recipes or guides must live on the same domain and CMS
- Who will install updates in twelve months

If the checklist is mostly blank, you do not have a platform problem yet. You have a scoping problem.

## Nuanced trade-offs

**Shopify strengths.** Less hosting burden, coherent checkout primitives, a large app ecosystem, and a merchant admin most teams can learn. Theme work can still be custom; it does not have to look like a preset.

**Shopify limits.** You rent the platform. Checkout and the data model are opinionated. Unusual B2B, membership or quoting logic can turn into an expensive app stack. Fighting the platform is usually a signal to change the requirement — or the platform.

**WooCommerce strengths.** WordPress content flexibility, host portability, and room for custom product types and flows. One CMS for store, pages and editorial is a genuine advantage when content sells the product.

**WooCommerce limits.** Quality varies with hosting and the plugin list. “It is free” is not the same as “it is cheap to run well”. A neglected WooCommerce store is a security and uptime problem, not a branding problem.

Apps and plugins both create hidden lock-in. A short, justified list — shipping, reviews, subscriptions only when you actually sell subscriptions — beats a store that cannot be explained without a spreadsheet of third-party tools.

## Catalogue, shipping and Australian operations

Perth retailers often underestimate fulfilment more than design. Western Australia shipping distances, click-and-collect, and split warehouse versus store stock change what you must show before checkout. Configure tax, payments and shipping to match how you actually fulfil — not how a theme demo is set up for another country.

If you sell both in person and online, decide where inventory is source of truth before you pick a theme. Platform choice will not rescue a messy stock process. Hospitality add-ons, appointments or course enrolment are different jobs again; do not stretch a cart into software it is not.

## Moving from another cart

A replatform is a [website migration](/services/website-migration/), not a visual refresh. Product data, customers, redirects and URL patterns all need a plan. If you also want a new look and information architecture, that is a separate [redesign](/services/website-redesign/) workstream. Mixing the two without saying so is how quotes become arguments.

Redirects protect search equity when product URLs change. Launch without them and you donate traffic to 404 pages. Launch timing is a checklist, not a date wish — [how long a website project takes](/blog/website-project-timeline/) covers where store builds actually stall, which is usually catalogue cleanup and content, not “the theme”.
