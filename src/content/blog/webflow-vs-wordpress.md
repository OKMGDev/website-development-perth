Webflow and WordPress both power strong marketing sites. For most Perth service businesses the deciding factors are not “which is more popular”, but who will edit the site, how opinionated your design system is, and which integrations you actually need — not which ones a plugin directory could theoretically provide.

If you are choosing a shopping cart, stop here and read [Shopify vs WooCommerce](/blog/shopify-vs-woocommerce/) instead. This article is about marketing and content sites: the kind of build we usually scope as [custom website design](/services/custom-website-design/) on one of these two stacks.

## Direct answer

Choose **Webflow** when design fidelity, a guarded visual system and a hosted CMS matter more than an enormous plugin ecosystem. Choose **WordPress** when editorial scale, familiar editing for non-designers, or a specific integration that already lives in the WordPress world matters more than pixel-level control in the designer.

Choose **neither** as a default if you need heavy governance, complex permissions and long-lived institutional content — that conversation often starts with [Drupal](/services/drupal-development/) — or if the “website” is really software, which belongs in [web application development](/services/web-application-development/).

The editor experience decides long-term success. A beautiful site that the marketing coordinator is afraid to touch will rot. A flexible site that anyone can break will look accidental within a year.

## What each platform is actually good at

**Webflow** is a design-first, hosted platform. Layout, interactions and a structured CMS live in one environment. That is why it excels when the visual system is part of the product: tight type, spacing, component states and marketing pages that should not drift. Hosting and SSL are part of the platform, which removes a class of “who patches the server” arguments.

**WordPress** is an open CMS with a vast ecosystem. It excels when many people publish, when you need a specific plugin or integration that already exists, or when the organisation already knows WordPress and will not retrain the whole office. You can build a disciplined design system in WordPress. You have to protect it — with a locked-down theme, a component library and rules about who may install plugins.

For delivery detail, see [Webflow website development](/services/webflow-development/) and [WordPress website development](/services/wordpress-development/).

## Editor experience: the question most comparisons skip

Ask who will change a heading on a Tuesday afternoon.

If the answer is a marketer who thinks in layout and is happy in a visual canvas, Webflow’s designer and Editor split can be a gift — provided you train them on *what* they may change. If the answer is several staff who only want a familiar post editor, WordPress still wins on comfort.

If the answer is “the intern, the director, and whoever has the password”, neither platform will save you. You need roles, a component model and [maintenance](/services/website-maintenance/) so plugin or Designer experiments do not become production.

[Professional services](/industries/professional-services/) firms in places such as [Subiaco](/locations/subiaco/) often fail this test: partners want control, marketers want speed, and nobody wants to look unprofessional. The CMS has to enforce the design system, not rely on taste.

### Editing checklist

- Named content owner per page type (home, services, team, articles)
- Fields they may edit versus layout they must not
- How images are cropped and compressed
- Who approves legal or claims-sensitive copy
- What happens when someone wants “just a popup”

If you cannot answer those, platform choice is premature.

## Design control versus plugin flexibility

Webflow’s strength is design fidelity: the site can match the intended [UX and UI](/services/ux-ui-design/) without fighting a theme’s opinions. The corresponding limit is ecosystem. Some third-party tools, membership setups or legacy integrations are simply easier in WordPress.

WordPress’s strength is that ecosystem and editorial scale: plugins, multilingual setups, complex blogging, and a hiring market that already speaks WordPress. The corresponding limit is discipline. Uncontrolled plugins create performance, security and visual drift. “We can do anything” becomes “we did everything, poorly”.

A useful rule: if the missing capability is a marketing integration with a documented API, either platform can usually connect. If the missing capability is “a plugin we saw in a blog post”, that is not a strategy.

## Integrations, SEO and content model

Both platforms can be fast, accessible and search-sensible when they are built that way. Neither is automatically good at [SEO](/services/seo-services/). Headings, internal links, redirects, structured content and performance are project choices.

Webflow’s CMS Collections reward a planned content model: services, locations, team, FAQs as types with fields, not as one-off pages. WordPress rewards the same planning, then stores it as custom post types and fields if you invest in that structure — or as a pile of pages if you do not.

Hospitality sites that change menus and events, such as operators we see around [Fremantle](/locations/fremantle/), often care more about *who can update a menu without a developer* than about the brand of CMS. That is an editor-experience requirement. Put it in the brief.

## When you are migrating, not choosing in a vacuum

Moving off Wix, Squarespace or an ageing WordPress theme is a [website migration](/services/website-migration/) if you are keeping the experience roughly intact, and a [redesign](/services/website-redesign/) if you are not. Do not let a platform preference launder a rebuild. Redirects, URL strategy and content inventory belong in scope either way.

If the current WordPress site is the problem, the honest question is whether WordPress was the wrong tool or whether the theme, plugins and hosting were neglected. Replatforming to Webflow to escape a mess you could have cleaned up is an expensive way to buy a fresh start — sometimes justified, often not.

## Decision framework

1. **Name the editors.** If they need a visual canvas and a tight system, lean Webflow. If they need a familiar post workflow and many contributors, lean WordPress.
2. **List integrations you cannot live without.** Confirm each one exists as a first-class connection, not a hope.
3. **Decide how much design drift you will tolerate.** Low drift favours Webflow or a strictly componentised WordPress theme. High volume of one-off landing layouts may still be WordPress with [campaign landing pages](/services/landing-page-design/) as a separate template.
4. **Look at governance.** Multiple brands, approval workflows or procurement rules may push you toward WordPress with a well-built theme — or away from both, toward Drupal.
5. **Price the care, not only the build.** Hosting, updates and small changes are part of the real cost. [Website cost in Perth](/blog/website-cost-perth/) is about those drivers, not a made-up average.

Platform choice also affects calendar. Design systems in Webflow and componentised WordPress both need content and feedback owners. [How long a website project takes](/blog/website-project-timeline/) is usually a content story, whichever CMS you pick.

## What this comparison is not

This is not a claim that one platform ranks better, converts better or is “more modern”. Those outcomes come from information architecture, content, performance and how the site is run. It is also not a substitute for a service page: if you already know the stack, go to the [Webflow](/services/webflow-development/) or [WordPress](/services/wordpress-development/) service and talk about delivery.

Examples in [our work](/work/) are useful once you can point at *why* a layout or CMS structure fits your editors. Mood boards without an editor plan produce pretty prisons.
